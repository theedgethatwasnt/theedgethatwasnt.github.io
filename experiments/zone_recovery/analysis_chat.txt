This is a great question about the aggregation properties of the Movement Efficiency Ratio (MER). The short answer is: neither simple addition nor geometric multiplication works, and the daily bar MER will systematically overstate efficiency compared to the hourly aggregation. Here's why:
Defining the Terms

For each hourly bar i:

    Net move = cᵢ − oᵢ
    Range = hᵢ − lᵢ (proxy for tape length)
    MERᵢ = (cᵢ − oᵢ) / (hᵢ − lᵢ)

For the 24-hour daily bar:

    MER_daily = (C₂₄ − O₁) / (H_max − L_min)

Why Addition Fails

The numerators do chain cleanly. Assuming closes roll into next opens (continuous price):

    Σ(cᵢ − oᵢ) = C₂₄ − O₁ ✓ (the daily net move)

But because each hourly net move is divided by its own range before summing, the sum of MERs is dimensionally incoherent as a ratio. You'd be adding fractions with different denominators and no common basis. The result has no clean geometric meaning.
Why Multiplication Fails

The product Π MERᵢ compounds ratios multiplicatively, which only makes sense for things like return factors (1 + r). MER is a signed efficiency scalar, not a growth factor. The product collapses toward zero quickly and doesn't map to any meaningful 24-hour efficiency quantity.
The Correct Aggregation

The right way to roll hourly MERs up to a 24-hour MER is a range-weighted average:

    MER₂₄ = Σ(cᵢ − oᵢ) / Σ(hᵢ − lᵢ)

Which can be rewritten as:

    MER₂₄ = Σ [ MERᵢ × (hᵢ − lᵢ) ] / Σ(hᵢ − lᵢ)

Each hour's MER is weighted by its own range (its contribution to total tape length). This is the natural, internally consistent aggregation.
Why the Daily Bar MER ≠ This

Here's the critical structural problem with comparing hourly to daily:

    Σ(hᵢ − lᵢ) ≥ (H_max − L_min)

The sum of hourly ranges is always greater than or equal to the daily bar's single range, because the daily high and low capture only the extremes, not the intraday back-and-forth between hours. The true tape length is best approximated by summing the hourly ranges — the daily H−L compresses this.

Since both use the same numerator (daily net move), but the daily bar uses a smaller denominator:

    MER_daily ≥ MER₂₄ (range-weighted)

The daily bar will systematically overstate efficiency. The more choppy and mean-reverting the intraday action, the larger this divergence.
Summary
Aggregation Method	Works?
Sum of hourly MERs	✗ — not meaningful
Product of hourly MERs	✗ — wrong framework
Daily bar (C−O)/(H−L)	✗ — biased upward, compresses tape
Range-weighted average of hourly MERs	✓ — correct aggregation

The practical implication: if you're comparing MER across timeframes, you need to be aware that higher-timeframe bars will always look more efficient than the sum of their parts, simply because the H−L range is a cruder (compressed) tape estimate. The hourly rollup with Σ(hᵢ − lᵢ) as the denominator is the more honest number.

Range-Weighted Average Formula

Explicitly written out:

That's it — just the sum of all 24 hourly net moves divided by the sum of all 24 hourly ranges. No weighting step needed separately; the weighting is implicit because you're pooling numerators and denominators before dividing. This is exactly equivalent to the weighted form and is the cleanest way to compute it.
On Tick Volume as the Denominator

This is a genuinely better idea in theory, and here's why:

The problem with H−L is that it's a spatial proxy for tape length — it only captures how far price stretched, not how much activity occurred. A quiet hour with one big spike has the same H−L as a violent hour with continuous movement.

Tick volume (number of transactions) is a temporal/activity proxy. It directly measures how much tape was printed.

So the ratio becomes:

    MER_vol = (cᵢ − oᵢ) / Vᵢ

where Vᵢ is tick count (or alternatively dollar volume).
Advantages

    Captures churning — a mean-reverting hour with huge volume correctly shows near-zero efficiency, whereas H−L might still look moderate
    More sensitive to genuine directionality vs noise
    Doesn't compress the way daily H−L does across timeframes — volumes add perfectly: Σ Vᵢ = V₂₄, so the 24-hour aggregation is exact with no bias

The aggregation property is now clean

    MER_vol_24 = Σ(cᵢ − oᵢ) / Σ Vᵢ = (C₂₄ − O₁) / V₂₄

Unlike the H−L version, this aggregates perfectly — the hourly rollup equals the daily bar calculation exactly, with no compression bias.
Disadvantages

    Tick volume is not always available (especially in FX or synthetic instruments)
    Raw tick count conflates trade size — a market with many small trades looks the same as fewer large ones, so dollar volume is usually preferable where available
    Sensitive to market microstructure changes (e.g., HFT activity inflates tick count without meaningful price discovery)

Which is Better?
Denominator	Tape Proxy Quality	Aggregation Property	Availability
H − L	Spatial, compressed	Biased across timeframes	Universal
Σ hourly (H−L)	Better spatial	Exact rollup	Universal
Tick count	Activity-based	Exact rollup	Often available
Dollar volume	Best overall	Exact rollup	Less universal

Dollar volume is the best proxy if you have it. Tick count is second. H−L is the fallback when volume data is absent. The key insight is that volume-based denominators solve the aggregation bias problem entirely, which is a meaningful structural advantage beyond just being a better tape estimate.

This is a rich structural problem. Let me build it up layer by layer.
The Core Math First

For any zone recovery system, the fundamental constraint is:

    Profit on escape leg ≥ Net loss on all accumulated positions

If zone width = Z, escape distance beyond zone edge = E, and positions accumulate with multiplier m at each zone crossing:

For a 2-side system after n crossings, the required escape profit must cover an asymmetric pyramid of losses. The breakeven escape distance is roughly:

    E = Z × (mⁿ − 1) / (mⁿ × (m − 1))

This means E shrinks relative to Z as n increases — counterintuitively, the more the market has ping-ponged, the less additional distance is needed to escape, because your accumulated position on the winning side becomes very large. But your drawdown exposure has grown geometrically.
Position Size Progression

Each zone penetration requires the new position to cover:

    All previous losing positions' open drawdown
    Plus a target profit on escape

The classic Joe Nemeth progression follows roughly a 1 : 2 : 3 : 5 : 8 Fibonacci-like structure, though implementations vary. What matters structurally:
Crossing #	Side	Cumulative Exposure	Notes
0	Long at bottom	1 unit	Initial trade
1	Short at top	2 units	Covers loss + breakeven
2	Long at bottom	3–4 units	Exposure accelerating
3	Short at top	6–8 units	Dangerous territory
4+	Either	12–20+ units	Margin risk critical

The practical implication: you need to define maximum crossing tolerance before you enter, not during the trade. The market you choose must have a high probability of escaping before crossing n times.
Zone Shape Analysis

This is the most original part of your question and genuinely underexplored.
1. Flat Horizontal Zone

Shape: Both borders are level parallel lines

Best case scenario mechanically. Zone width is constant, so:

    Each crossing costs the same Z pips of adverse movement
    Position progression is predictable and calculable in advance
    Escape distance is symmetric — same pip requirement in either direction

Best market: Mean-reverting, bracketed by clear support/resistance, low trending bias. Works best in ranging sessions like Tokyo or pre-news consolidation.

Risk: Works until it suddenly doesn't — a breakout that doesn't return.
2. Sloped/Trending Zone

Shape: Both borders are parallel but tilted — zone moves with the trend

This is intellectually more honest because you're acknowledging directional bias. The zone drifts in trend direction, so:

    Escape in trend direction requires less absolute price movement because the zone edge is moving toward price
    Escape against trend requires more movement because you're fighting the slope
    Position accumulation is asymmetric — trend-direction crossings are fewer and cheaper

Best market: A slow, measured trend with periodic retracements — not a volatile impulse trend. EURUSD in a macro drift environment. The slope should roughly match the dominant regression slope of price.

Risk: If trend accelerates, the zone can't keep up and you get trapped outside.
3. Divergent Zone (Starts Narrow, Widens)

Shape: Bottom and top borders spread apart as time progresses — megaphone / expanding triangle

This is the most dangerous zone shape and here's why:

    Early crossings are cheap because Z is narrow at the start
    As time progresses, each crossing costs more pips than the last
    The position size progression AND the zone width are both growing — a compounding of compounding
    Escape distance also grows over time because the zone edge is farther away

The math is punishing: your required escape distance grows faster than your position size benefit accumulates. The system degrades with time.

Best use: Only if you intend to close the system early — exploit the cheap initial crossings and exit before the zone widens. Useful for modeling increasing volatility environments (pre-news, early London session) where you know volatility will expand.
4. Convergent Zone (Starts Wide, Narrows)

Shape: Borders compress toward each other — wedge or triangle shape

This is structurally the most favorable zone shape for recovery, and here's why:

    Early crossings are expensive in pip terms (wide Z) but accumulate large positions quickly
    As the zone narrows, later crossings are cheaper — price doesn't need to travel as far to cross
    The required escape distance shrinks as the zone converges because the zone edge is moving toward the center
    The system naturally forces resolution — a convergent zone mathematically must produce a breakout

Best market: A consolidating market that you believe is coiling toward a breakout — falling volatility, compression before an event, Bollinger Band squeeze conditions. The narrowing zone models the natural compression of price action.

Risk: If the breakout is delayed and crossings accumulate in the wide early phase, drawdown can be severe before the beneficial narrowing kicks in.

Flat horizontal	Constant	Symmetric, fixed	Range-bound, clear S/R	Sudden breakout
Sloped trending	Constant	Asymmetric, trend favored	Slow measured trend	Trend acceleration
Divergent (widens)	Increasing	Growing, deteriorating	Early volatility expansion	Time decay of math
Convergent (narrows)	Decreasing	Shrinking, improving	Coiling pre-breakout	Large early drawdown
The Market Characterization Checklist

To select the right market and zone shape, you want to measure:

1. Volatility regime — ATR relative to its own history. Low and falling ATR favors convergent zones. Rising ATR favors divergent only for early exits.

2. Autocorrelation of returns — Positive autocorrelation (trending) favors sloped zones. Negative autocorrelation (mean-reverting) favors flat horizontal zones.

3. Time-to-breakout expectation — How long does this pair typically consolidate before escaping? This sets your maximum crossing tolerance. Pairs with shorter consolidation cycles are more forgiving.

4. Session behavior — Flat zones work in Tokyo/London pre-open. Sloped zones work in NY trend hours. Avoid zone recovery entirely in news-driven volatility — the zone shape is undefined.

5. The ratio Z/E — Your zone width should be wide enough that random noise doesn't trigger unnecessary crossings, but narrow enough that the required escape distance E is reachable within a reasonable move. Practically, E should be no more than 1.5–2× Z for the system to be viable before margin becomes critical.

What Changes Structurally

With E = 10Z, the escape target is 10 zone-widths beyond the zone edge. The immediate mechanical consequence is:

The position progression becomes much flatter. Because the escape leg travels so far, even a modest accumulated position on that side generates enough profit to cover all the losses. You don't need aggressive Fibonacci scaling. The math lets you be gentle on progression precisely because the payoff leg is so long.

But here is the critical counterweight:
The Probability Problem

For a random walk, the probability of price reaching escape target E before returning to the zone is approximately:

    P(escape) ≈ Z / (Z + E)

If E = 10Z:

    P(escape) ≈ 1/11 ≈ 9%

This is not a minor adjustment. You are designing a system that escapes cleanly less than 1 in 10 times. The other 9 times, price oscillates back into the zone, adds another crossing, and the system continues accumulating.
The System Has Become Something Else Entirely

A 10:1 E/Z ratio transforms zone recovery from a mean-reversion capture tool into something that structurally resembles a long straddle or synthetic strangle:
Feature	Classic Zone Recovery (E ≈ 1-2Z)	Wide Escape (E = 10Z)
Win frequency	High	Very low (~9%)
Win size	Small	Very large
Position progression	Aggressive	Shallow/flat
Duration per trade	Short	Very long
Cost of waiting	Low (few crossings)	High (many crossings)
Payoff profile	Income-like	Lottery-like
Analogous to	Selling options	Buying options

The system is now long a large directional move. You accumulate positions on both sides while the market oscillates inside the zone, and you profit when it finally breaks hard in one direction. You are essentially synthesizing a long volatility position through spot FX mechanics.
The Hidden Costs That Become Critical

With short E/Z, positions are held briefly and swap costs are negligible. With E = 10Z, positions sit open for potentially days or weeks. Now the system has a continuous cost of carry:

Swap/rollover on accumulated positions in both directions becomes a meaningful drag. Depending on the pair and the direction of your carry exposure, this can be significantly negative. On a pair like USDJPY where the swap differential is large, accumulated positions over many days can erode a substantial portion of your theoretical escape profit.

Spread cost per crossing also accumulates. If Z is small (which is one reason E/Z is large), the zone is crossed frequently, and each crossing costs a spread. With 15–20 crossings before escape, you may have paid 15–20 spreads before seeing any profit.
The Margin Exposure Is Deceptive

Here is the subtle danger: because progression is flat, the margin per crossing looks manageable. But because the system runs much longer before escaping, you are simultaneously:

    Holding more open positions
    For longer duration
    On both sides of the book

The peak margin exposure may not be dramatically different from an aggressive progression system, but it arrives slowly and invisibly. Traders underestimate it because each individual crossing doesn't look alarming.
When This Design Makes Sense

Despite all of the above, there are specific conditions where 10:1 E/Z is the right design:

1. You have identified a high-probability large move incoming If you have a macro thesis that EURUSD is going to move 200+ pips in one direction within a defined timeframe — central bank divergence, major event risk — then designing a wide escape captures that move while the zone handles the noise beforehand.

2. Positive carry on the escape direction If the escape direction happens to be the positive carry direction for that pair, the swap income partially offsets your waiting cost and even adds to your escape profit.

3. Very low crossing frequency If Z is large rather than small, and E is 10× that large Z, then crossings are rare and spread/swap costs are minimal. The system sits quietly with very little activity, waiting for the big move. This requires substantial capital but is operationally clean.

4. Convergent zone shape A converging zone combined with large E/Z is potentially the most coherent combination — the zone is narrowing (naturally forcing a breakout) and the escape is generous enough that when the breakout comes, it covers everything comfortably. The narrowing zone increases breakout probability while the large E captures the resulting move fully.
The Core Insight

The E/Z ratio is essentially a dial between two fundamentally different strategies:

    Low E/Z → Mean reversion capture, income profile, high win rate, aggressive progression

    High E/Z → Volatility event capture, lottery profile, low win rate, flat progression

At 10:1, you have completely crossed from one side to the other. Neither is wrong — but applying a 10:1 E/Z system to a quiet ranging market is a slow bleed, and applying a 1:1 E/Z system to a trending volatile market is a margin call.

The E/Z ratio should be calibrated to the ratio of the market's typical oscillation amplitude to its typical breakout magnitude. If the pair typically ranges 30 pips intraday and breaks out 300 pips when it trends, then 10:1 is actually the correct natural ratio for that instrument. You are simply mirroring the market's own geometry back at it.

The Core Principle

ATR measures the typical bar oscillation. You want:

    Z large enough that random noise doesn't trigger unnecessary crossings
    Z small enough that crossings are meaningful when they occur
    E proportional to the market's typical trending move, not its oscillation

These are two different ATR measurements — oscillation and trend magnitude — and that distinction is the key insight.
The Two ATRs You Need

ATR_short — 5 to 8 period ATR on your working timeframe. Captures current noise and oscillation amplitude. This calibrates Z.

ATR_long — 50 to 100 period ATR on a higher timeframe, or alternatively the average impulsive move length when the market does trend. This calibrates E.

The ratio ATR_long / ATR_short is essentially the market telling you its natural E/Z ratio.
Recommended Calibration
Zone Width Z

    Z = 0.5 × ATR_short (M5 or H1 depending on timeframe)

The 0.5 multiplier is critical. You want Z to be below the typical bar range so that genuine penetrations require committed price action, not just a routine bar extension. If Z equals a full ATR, almost nothing triggers a crossing. If Z equals 0.25 ATR, noise triggers crossings constantly.

Practical ranges by timeframe:

M5	8 period M5 ATR	0.4 – 0.6

H4	5 period H4 ATR	0.6 – 0.8

    Resume or close depending on whether the expansion is a genuine regime change or a news spike

Phase 5: Position Size Progression — The Core Math

The Breakeven Constraint

For any zone recovery system, escape after leg n on the long side must satisfy:

    Profit_long_side ≥ Loss_short_side + All_spread_costs

Long positions accumulate at zone bottom (odd legs: 1, 3, 5...)
Short positions accumulate at zone top (even legs: 2, 4, 6...)

At escape going long, each long position profits E pips and each short position loses Z pips:

    (B₁ + B₃ + B₅...) × E ≥ (B₂ + B₄ + B₆...) × Z + n × spread × avg_position

This constraint must hold for every n from 1 to max_legs. This is the system of constraints that the Lagrange optimization solves simultaneously.
Framework 1: Martingale (Baseline for Comparison)

Progression: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512

Total exposure at 10 legs: 1,023B

Meets the breakeven constraint trivially but at catastrophic capital cost. This is the mathematical ceiling — everything else is measured against it.

    Σ Bₙ = n(n+1)/2 × B

The system geometry and the position sizing are not independent — they are co-dependent.
Framework 3: Lagrange-Optimized Convex Progression

The optimization problem formally stated:

    Minimize: Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ Bᵢ (total capital exposure)

The general result takes the form:

    Bₙ ∝ n^α × B

Where α is derived from the optimization and is approximately 1.5 for E/Z ratios in the 8–12 range typical of major FX pairs.

The convex progression front-loads the constraint satisfaction so that fewer legs are needed on average, which is the real source of the capital efficiency.
